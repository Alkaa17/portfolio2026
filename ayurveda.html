product & growth design

Designing for growth across the customer journey

the challenge

Growth wasn’t simply about
getting people to click “Buy.”

The Ayurveda Experience sells Ayurvedic wellness and skincare products. Buying was rarely impulsive; it was research-heavy.

Before converting, customers looked for education, credibility, ingredient and benefit details, reviews and reassurance. Taking those away to “speed things up” risked taking away the reasons to buy.

The brief was not

“Make checkout shorter.”

The real design question

How might we increase conversion and retention without removing the education and trust customers needed to make confident purchase decisions?

target audience

Designing for the women at the heart of the brand

Most of our customers were women aged 45 to 70 across the United States and Canada, often reading long pages on a phone. They weren't an edge case. They were the core audience, so accessibility wasn't a separate workstream; it was how every page, email and app screen in this case study was designed. As readability improved, engagement and conversion rose with it.

That meant choosing clarity over trend: high-contrast color and photography instead of the neutral pastel look of modern aesthetics, type no smaller than 18px, and bigger CTAs.

  • Larger typeBody copy never below 18px, with 22px as the paragraph default.
  • High contrastStrong, saturated color in place of soft pastels and neutral, minimal palettes.
  • Clear photographyBright, high-contrast product and lifestyle photos that read at a glance.
  • Bigger targetsLarge CTAs and controls sized for real hands on real phones.
  • ReadabilityContent density and sectioning that make long pages easy to scan.
  • Content structureClear hierarchy so the key point is found first.
  • SpacingRoom to breathe between dense blocks of information.
  • Responsive behaviorLayouts that keep their meaning on small screens.
  • Cognitive loadOne decision at a time, with buttons that say exactly what happens next.

Optimization 01

Buy box clarity

The decision point: convert or drop off

Observed
The buy box is the decision point. Shoppers arrive after reading or skimming the whole page, and they either convert here or leave. If one element was missing, like the price saving, a size, payment options or the returns policy, that gap could quietly push someone to drop off. On pages where the buy box was missing an element, drop-off rose by 9%.
Hypothesis
If the buy box clearly answered every question a shopper has at the moment of decision, the only reason left to leave would be the product itself, not the experience.
Design change
Each buy box told shoppers exactly what they would get, concisely and in the same order: the full name and what it does, ticked benefits, size, offer, price with the saving shown, quantity options, a prominent add to cart, then payment and returns. Product images matched the email and landing page that brought people there, and older products showed their reviews.
Result / learning
Conversion from long-form landing page to cart improved significantly across every long-form page.

Optimization 02

Cart and checkout

Friction · purchase path · checkout continuation

Observed
After clicking add to cart on a landing page's buy box, shoppers were sent to the product page first, then to the cart, then to checkout.
Hypothesis
Shoppers used to Amazon expect to buy in about three steps, so every extra step added friction. Someone who has read a whole long-form page and clicked the green CTA has already decided. Sending them through a product page first was a distraction at the moment intent was highest.
Design change
Add to cart on the landing page now takes people straight to the cart, cutting a full step out of the purchase path.
Result / learning
Drop-off in that flow fell by 17% in the first month.

A tracking page has one primary job

“Where is my order?”

Customers returned to it often, making it one of the highest-intent post-purchase touchpoints. The existing page showed shipment status and little else. The goal was to protect that core task while supporting confidence, support, discovery, app adoption, and repeat engagement.

New page order, by priority

  1. Order visibilityShipment status, immediately.
  2. Product discoveryBestsellers and relevant products.
  3. Trust buildingCustomer testimonials.
  4. Support accessCustomer care, without hunting.
  5. Retention channelsAn invitation to the app.

Business opportunities were introduced only after the customer's immediate need was satisfied.

Mobile app

Expanding the strategy into mobile

The app carried the same lifecycle thinking into a channel built for repeat visits: a redesigned navigation, stronger collection pages, promotional modules, and notifications that brought people back with app-exclusive offers.

Notification strategy

Small, fun pushes that gave people a reason to open the app

We started a small notification strategy to lift engagement among app users. Every push was treated as a tiny campaign, not a broadcast.

05

Optimize

Testing decisions instead of defending assumptions

Not every test produced a winner. The ones that didn't still changed what was designed next.

Experiment 01 · Mobile app

Side navigation vs. bottom navigation

Signal

The apps we all use every day, and most other ecommerce brands, had moved to a bottom navigation bar. Ours still hid everything behind a side drawer.

Hypothesis

Keeping the main destinations always visible would make simple tasks faster. From a business lens, it would also give shoppers a one-tap route into Collections to keep browsing, instead of searching for a specific product or digging through the tabs. Buttons were placed for thumb reach, assuming most people use the phone right-handed or with both hands.

Experiment 02 · Landing page and email

CTA size, copy, placement, color and interaction

Signal

The CTA is the one element every page and email shares, and shoppers kept scrolling past it. Each property of the button was tested on its own, so we'd know which one actually moved clicks.

Hypothesis

For an older audience, a bigger, high-contrast button with clear copy, repeated where the decision happens and gently drawing the eye on scroll, would earn more clicks than a small, quiet one.

Experiment 03 · Long-form landing page

How to show ingredients

Signal

Ingredients are why people buy Ayurveda. On long-form pages, the ingredient section is where shoppers check what's inside and what each ingredient does for them.

Hypothesis

Cut-out ingredients on white, labeled with their benefits and an icon, would be easier to scan and tie each ingredient to an outcome faster than full square photos.

Small optimizations compounded into meaningful growth

Customers came to buy Ayurvedic products only after reading up on ingredients, benefits, reviews and reassurance, so the easy fix of making pages shorter would have removed the reasons to buy. Instead of cutting content, I kept the education and trust signals and made them easier to find, test and act on across the site, checkout and the app.
